Section 9.1

Beyond the sine wave

Periodic and nonperiodic signals, and the four shapes in Lab 9.

Lab 5B used a transient analysis with a sine wave. Many circuits, especially digital ones, are driven by other shapes. Lab 9 feeds four of them into the same simple RC circuit and watches what comes out.

Periodic and nonperiodic signals

  • A periodic waveform repeats the same shape over and over. Its period is the time for one cycle, and its frequency is 1 ÷ period.
  • A nonperiodic waveform, such as random noise from electronics or lightning, never repeats in a pattern. This course sticks to periodic signals.

Predicting how a circuit responds to these shapes involves exponential functions that are tedious by hand. That's exactly what a simulator is for.

Section 9.2

The pulse source

Seven fields that make pulses, squares, triangles and ramps.

One part makes all four shapes: the pulse source (Pulse on the toolbar; VPULSE in PSpice). Instead of a single value, it has seven fields in the Selected part panel.

Pulse source fields
FieldMeaning
V1The low level: before the first pulse and between pulses.
V2The high level, during the pulse.
TDDelay from time 0 to the start of the first rise.
TRRise time: how long it takes to go from V1 to V2.
TFFall time: how long it takes to return from V2 to V1.
PWPulse width: how long it stays at V2.
PERPeriod: the time for one full cycle, after which the pattern repeats.

Pulse builder

Load one of the lab's sources, or type your own values (SPICE suffixes work). The drawing marks each field on the waveform.

Useful numbers

  • Frequency = 1 ÷ PER.
  • Duty cycle = time spent high ÷ PER. For a symmetric square wave it's 50%.
  • Shape comes from TR, PW and TF. Tiny TR and TF give sharp edges; long, equal TR and TF give a triangle; a long TR and a tiny TF give a ramp.

Section 9.3

The RC circuit and its time constant

Why a capacitor lags, and how RC sets the pace.

Every Lab 9 circuit is the same shape: the source, then R1 in series, then C1 to ground. What changes is how fast the input moves compared with how fast C1 can follow.

Select a part or the probes.

The time constant

A capacitor's voltage can't jump. Current has to flow through R1 to charge it, and the bigger R and C are, the slower that is. The time constant measures that speed:

τ = R × C

  • After 1 τ the capacitor has covered 63% of the change.
  • After 5 τ it has covered over 99%: it's effectively done.
  • While it charges, the rest of the voltage is across R1, so V(IN,OUT) is the part C1 hasn't caught up with yet.

Charging a capacitor

A voltage step is applied at time 0. Choose R and C and watch how the capacitor's voltage follows.

Reading 9A and 9B: pulses

  • At the rising edge, IN jumps from 0.3 V to 4.6 V but OUT can't move instantly, so the whole 4.3 V jump appears across R1: v(in,out) spikes up.
  • During the pulse, C1 charges; OUT rises and v(in,out) decays back to zero within about 5τ.
  • At the falling edge, the opposite happens: v(in,out) spikes down, then decays.
  • 9B is the same picture compressed about ten times in time. Compare the time axes.

Reading 9C and 9D: slopes

  • On a steady slope, C1 needs a steady current to keep up, so R1's voltage settles at a constant value: RC × slope. That's why v(in,out) looks nearly square in 9C.
  • In 9D, the slow ramp gives a small, steady v(in,out). The fast drop is almost like an edge, so v(in,out) swings sharply negative.
  • OUT lags IN by about one time constant, and in 9C it never quite reaches the peak before the input turns around.

Predict first. Lab 9C: the input rises 4.3 V in 49.5 µs, and RC = 6.8 µs. What steady voltage does v(in,out) settle at on the rising slope?

  1. Slope = 4.3 V ÷ 49.5 µs = 86,900 V/s.
  2. RC × slope = 0.0000068 × 86,900 = 0.59 V.
  3. On the falling slope it's the same size but negative.

Section 9.5

Framing and measuring results

Axis ranges, the Measurements table, time steps, and common fixes.

Transient plots are only useful if they're framed and read carefully. These habits apply to every lab from here on.

Framing: the one-division rule

The handout asks that the waveforms come within one grid division of the top and bottom of the plot. In Q Circuits, open Axis ranges under the plot and set a Y range just outside the traces. For Lab 9A, for example, the traces run from about −4.3 V to 4.6 V, so −5 to 5 works. Leave X blank to see the whole run.

Measuring: the Measurements table

  • The Measurements table under the plot lists each trace's maximum and minimum, among other values.
  • It covers only the visible X range. If you set an X range, or drag across the plot to select a region, the numbers change to match. Clear X to measure the whole run.
  • Read the row for v(in,out), not V(IN) or V(OUT).

Choosing the time step

Each lab's step is about one thousandth of its stop time. That's small enough to capture the fastest edge. A larger step can jump right over a nanosecond edge and underreport the spike in v(in,out).

Dotted-underlined words in the lesson show their definition when you point at, tap or tab to them. The full course glossary (opens in a new tab) is searchable.

Periodic / nonperiodic
Repeating regularly, or not.
Period
Time for one cycle; 1 ÷ frequency.
Pulse source
A source set by V1, V2, TD, TR, TF, PW and PER.
Rise time / fall time
How long the pulse takes to go up or come down.
Pulse width
How long the pulse stays high.
Duty cycle
Fraction of each period spent high.

Max, min and more for each trace over the visible range.
