Section 8.1

AC signals and frequency response

Gain, Bode plots, and why capacitors and inductors respond to frequency.

Lab 5 introduced the AC sweep with a finished circuit. In Lab 8 you'll draw your own AC circuits, add an inductor's cousin, the transformer, and learn to frame the result so it's easy to read.

Gain against frequency

An AC sweep answers one question: how much of the input reaches the output at each frequency? That ratio is the gain. Plotted in decibels against frequency on a logarithmic scale, it's called a Bode plot. Three features matter on every Bode plot:

  • The flat region, where the gain barely changes.
  • The critical frequency, where the gain has dropped 3 dB below the flat region.
  • The roll-off, where the gain keeps falling. A simple RC circuit falls 20 dB for every decade of frequency.

Why parts respond to frequency

A resistor opposes current by the same amount at every frequency. Capacitors and inductors don't: their opposition, called reactance, changes with frequency, in opposite directions.

CapacitorXC = 1 ÷ (2πfC)Very large: acts like an open circuitVery small: acts like a wire
InductorXL = 2πfLVery small: acts like a wireVery large: acts like an open circuit

Reactance explorer

Both axes are logarithmic, so the reactances are straight lines. Where a line crosses the resistor's line, that part and the resistor have equal opposition: that's a critical frequency.

SPICE numbers, one more time

Lab 8 uses values from 1 µΩ to 1,000 MΩ. Remember the scale letters from Lab 4: 1u is 0.000001, 10m is 0.01, 1000MEG is 1,000,000,000. And the trap: M means milli, so write MEG for millions.

Section 8.2

New parts and plot controls

Inductors, transformers, and the Axis ranges and dB controls under the plot.

Lab 8 adds two parts and two plot controls to what you already know.

Inductor

Toolbar: L

A coil of wire. It opposes changes in current, so its reactance rises with frequency. Measured in henries (H); lab values use m and u.

More about the inductor

How it works. Current through a coil creates a magnetic field. When the current tries to change, the changing field pushes back with a voltage that opposes the change. The faster the change (the higher the frequency), the stronger the push-back.

  • Reactance: XL = 2πfL. A 10 mH inductor is about 0.6 Ω at 10 Hz but 6.3 kΩ at 100 kHz.
  • At DC an ideal inductor is just a wire.
  • Uses: filters, power supplies, radio tuning, and, when two coils share a field, transformers.

Transformer

Toolbar: Xfmr

Two coils (windings) sharing a magnetic core. AC in the primary produces AC in the secondary, with no wire between them.

More about the transformer

How it works. The changing current in the primary makes a changing magnetic field. The core carries that field through the secondary, where it induces a voltage.

  • Three settings: L1_VALUE (primary inductance), L2_VALUE (secondary inductance) and COUPLING (0 to 1, how much field the two coils share).
  • Voltage ratio: about coupling × √(L2 ÷ L1). If L2 is larger it's a step-up transformer; if smaller, step-down; if equal, an isolation transformer.
  • Dots mark the ends that are in step (in phase): pins 1 and 3.
  • Isolation: the two sides share no wire, so each side needs its own path to ground. That's why Lab 8D needs RD.

AC test source

Toolbar: V, DC 0, AC magnitude 1

The same source as Lab 5A. In PSpice it's VAC set to 1Vac and 0Vdc.

More about the AC test source
  • Keep the AC magnitude at 1 V so the output in dB is the gain in dB.
  • For a transient run you'd use a SIN( ) value or a Pulse source instead (Lab 9).

Plot controls

Under the Q Circuits plot

Axis ranges set fixed X and Y limits. The Magnitude dB / Magnitude / Phase buttons choose how an AC result is shown.

More about the plot controls
  • Axis ranges is PSpice's User Defined data range. Leave a pair blank for automatic; SPICE suffixes work (10k). In dB mode, the Y range is in dB.
  • Traces are cut off at the frame, and the Measurements table covers only the visible X range.
  • Lab 8's checks look at your axis ranges and that the plot is in Magnitude dB, which is how it opens.
  • Q Circuits draws the 10 minor divisions between decades automatically, so the handout's X Grid step isn't needed.

Section 8.3

Lab 8A: a low-pass Bode plot

Draw an RC circuit, sweep it, and frame the plot to read the critical frequency.

Open 8A · AC sweep of an RC circuit. The sheet is blank: draw the handout's series RC circuit, sweep it, and frame the Bode plot to read the critical frequency.

Select a part or the probe.

What the circuit does

R1 and C1 form a voltage divider, but C1's share changes with frequency. At low frequencies C1's reactance is huge, so it takes nearly all of the input and B follows A: the flat region. At high frequencies C1's reactance is small, so R1 takes most of the input and B falls: the roll-off. That's a low-pass filter.

Draw and set up

  1. Type LAB 08A in the circuit name box.
  2. Place R1 = 1k across the top, C1 = 1u upright below and right of it, and VS on the left with + up: value DC 0, AC magnitude 1.
  3. Wire the loop, ground the bottom wire, and add net aliases A (between VS and R1) and B (between R1 and C1).
  4. In the Analysis panel choose AC sweep: Start 1, Stop 100k, 201 points per decade.
  5. Probe B. The AC plot opens in Magnitude dB.
  6. Run. Open Axis ranges under the plot and set Y from -20 to 0, and X from 1 to 10k.
  7. Hover the plot, find the frequency where the gain is 3 dB below the flat part, and enter it under Your readings. Then press Check my work.

Why the lab frames it this way

  • Y from −20 to 0 dB shows the flat region at the top and exactly 20 dB of roll-off: one decade's worth.
  • X from 1 Hz to 10 kHz removes the empty right-hand side, so the interesting part fills the plot.
  • The minor gridlines between decades (2, 3, 4 … 9) let you read the frequency precisely.

The handout reads the critical frequency as about 161 Hz from its plot. The exact value is 1 ÷ (2πRC); your reading from the plot should be close to it.

Predict first. Work out the critical frequency, 1 ÷ (2πRC), for R1 = 1k and C1 = 1u, in hertz.

f = 1 ÷ (2 × 3.1416 × 1000 × 0.000001) = 1 ÷ 0.0062832 = 159 Hz.

Section 8.4

Lab 8B: the voltage across R1

Use a differential probe to see the high-pass half of the same circuit.

Open 8B · The voltage across R1. It offers to copy your 8A circuit. This time you measure across R1 with a differential probe, the other half of the same voltage divider.

Select the probe or a part.

What to expect

R1's share is the opposite of C1's. At low frequencies C1 takes almost everything, so V(A,B) is tiny. At high frequencies R1 takes almost everything, so V(A,B) rises to 0 dB. That's a high-pass filter, and its critical frequency is the same 1 ÷ (2πRC). In the explorer above, tick both probes: the two curves cross at the critical frequency, where each is 3 dB down.

  1. Start from your 8A circuit and rename it LAB 08B.
  2. Remove the probe on B: pick Probe and click it again.
  3. Pick Diff probe (X). Click A first, then B. The trace is v(a,b).
  4. Keep the 8A sweep and run. In Magnitude dB mode every trace is already in dB, so the handout's DB( ) trace edit isn't needed.
  5. Set the axis ranges: Y from -20 to 0, X from 10 to 10k.
  6. Enter the frequency where V(A,B) is 3 dB below its high-frequency level, then press Check my work.

Why the 3 dB points match. At the critical frequency, R1 and XC are equal. Each then gets 70.7% of the input, not 50%, because the two voltages are 90° out of step and add like the sides of a right triangle.

Section 8.5

Lab 8C: sweeping the capacitor

Step C1 through five values and watch the critical frequency move.

Open 8C · Sweeping the capacitor. As in Lab 7C, a parameter lets one run cover several values, this time for C1.

Select the Parameter part or C1.

  1. Start from your 8A circuit and rename it LAB 08C.
  2. Change C1's value to {CVAL}, curly braces included.
  3. Place a Parameter part anywhere: name CVAL, value 1u.
  4. Keep the AC sweep and tick Parametric sweep: parameter CVAL, Linear, Start 1u, End 3u, Increment 0.5u.
  5. Keep the probe on B and the 8A axis ranges (Y −20 to 0, X 1 to 10k), then run. You'll see five curves.
  6. Enter the critical frequency for the smallest and the largest capacitor, then press Check my work.

Section 8.6

Lab 8D: adding a transformer

Step the signal up with a transformer, and give the floating side a path to ground.

Open 8D · Adding a transformer. You'll put a step-up transformer between the source and the 8C filter.

Choose a part of the circuit to highlight it, or select a part.

Select a part for its settings and purpose.

Two small resistors with big jobs

  • RS (1 µΩ) sits between VS and the primary. An ideal source wired straight across an ideal inductor is a combination SPICE can't solve; a one-millionth-of-an-ohm resistor fixes that without changing the answer.
  • RD (1000 MΩ) connects the primary side to ground. The transformer passes AC, but no DC path crosses it, so without RD the primary side is floating. RD is so large it draws essentially no current.

Transformer explorer

Build it in Q Circuits

  1. Start from your 8C circuit and rename it LAB 08D.
  2. Cut VS (⌘X) and paste it about an inch to the left. Place the Transformer (Xfmr) in the gap: primary (left pins) toward VS, secondary (right pins) toward R1. Name it TX1.
  3. In the Selected part panel set L1_VALUE 10u, L2_VALUE 10m, COUPLING 0.975.
  4. Place RS = 1u between A (VS +) and the top primary pin (pin 1). Wire VS − to the bottom primary pin (pin 2).
  5. Wire the top secondary pin (3) to R1 and the bottom secondary pin (4) to the ground wire under C1.
  6. Place RD = 1000MEG from VS − to its own ground.
  7. Keep the AC and parametric sweeps and the probe on B. Run, then set the Y range to 10 to 30: the output is now above the input.
  8. Enter the low-frequency gain at B for CVAL = 1u, in dB, then press Check my work. The lab also checks that the primary side reaches ground only through RD.

In the capacitor explorer in section 8.5, choose 8D: with transformer to see the same five curves lifted by the transformer's gain.

Predict first. Estimate the low-frequency gain in dB: 20 × log₁₀(coupling × √(L2 ÷ L1)), with L1 = 10u, L2 = 10m and coupling 0.975.

  1. L2 ÷ L1 = 0.01 ÷ 0.00001 = 1,000.
  2. √1000 = 31.6, and 0.975 × 31.6 = 30.8.
  3. 20 × log₁₀(30.8) = 29.8 dB, which is why the lab sets the Y axis from 10 to 30 dB.

Glossary

Dotted-underlined words in the lesson show their definition when you point at, tap or tab to them. The full course glossary (opens in a new tab) is searchable.

Bode plot
Gain in dB against frequency on a log axis.
Critical frequency
Where the gain is 3 dB below its flat level.
Roll-off
The steady fall in gain beyond the critical frequency.
Decade
A factor of ten in frequency.
Reactance
A capacitor's or inductor's opposition to AC.
Inductor
A coil whose reactance rises with frequency.
Transformer
Coupled coils that pass AC between two circuits.
Primary / secondary
The source-side and load-side windings.
Coupling
How much magnetic field the windings share, 0 to 1.
Axis ranges
Fixed X and Y limits for the plot.
