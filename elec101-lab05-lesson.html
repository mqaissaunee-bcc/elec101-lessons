Section 5.1

Simulations and analysis types

The three steps of a simulation in Q Circuits, the four analysis types, and how to pick one.

In Lab 4 you mostly drew and repaired schematics. From Lab 5 on, the schematic becomes the input to a simulation: the simulator solves the circuit's equations and shows what a real circuit would do, without building it. In Q Circuits a simulation takes three steps.

  1. Choose what to calculate in the Analysis panel: the analysis type and its settings.
  2. Choose what to show with the Probe tool (B). Each probe becomes a trace on the plot.
  3. Press Run simulation. Results appear in the plot below the sheet.

  • Click a wire with Probe (B) to graph that node's voltage, measured from ground, for example V(OUT). In an AC sweep it's shown in decibels.
  • Click a part's body to graph the current through it, for example i(r1).

Section 5.2

Lab 5A: new parts and ideas

Op amps, capacitors, AC sources, and the decibel.

Lab 5A's circuit, lab05a, is an amplifier that boosts a middle band of frequencies and cuts very low and very high ones. It introduces three new kinds of part.

Operational amplifier

Toolbar: LM324 op-amp (5 pins)

A triangle with two inputs (+ and −) and one output. It amplifies the difference between its inputs. Resistors around it set the gain. The two short leads on top and bottom are supply pins.

More about the operational amplifier

How it works. On its own, an op amp has enormous gain (around 100,000 for the LM324). That's far too much to use directly, so a feedback network of resistors sends part of the output back to the − input. The feedback settles the gain at a precise, useful value set only by the resistors.

Two rules of thumb for an op amp with feedback:

  • The output adjusts until the + and − inputs are at (almost) the same voltage.
  • Almost no current flows into either input.
  • Output limits: the output can't swing beyond its supply voltages; in practice it stops a little short of them. Ask for more and the waveform flattens (clips).
  • Speed limit: the LM324's gain-bandwidth is about 1 MHz. At a gain of 3 it works well up to roughly 330 kHz, far above this lab's 100 kHz sweep.
  • Real part: an LM324 chip contains four op amps in one 14-pin package, sharing one pair of supply pins.

Capacitor

Toolbar: C

Two parallel plates. It blocks steady DC and lets changing signals through, more easily at higher frequencies. Values use u (micro) and n (nano) farads.

More about the capacitor

How it works. A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator. Current can't flow straight through, but charge builds up on one plate and pushes an equal charge off the other. A steady voltage charges it once and then nothing more flows; a changing voltage keeps charging and discharging it, so signal gets through.

Reactance. A capacitor's opposition to AC is its reactance, XC = 1 ÷ (2πfC). It falls as frequency rises. For Lab 5A's C1 (0.15 µF), XC is about 106 kΩ at 10 Hz but only about 106 Ω at 10 kHz.

  • Filters: with a resistor it forms a filter. The corner frequency, 1 ÷ (2πRC), is where XC equals R.
  • Units: a farad is huge, so real values are in µF (u), nF (n) and pF (p). 1 µF = 1,000 nF.
  • Types: small ceramic capacitors work either way round. Electrolytic capacitors hold larger values but are polarized: fitted backwards, they can fail.

AC test source

Toolbar: V, with AC magnitude 1

The signal source for an AC sweep. Its AC magnitude sets the amplitude; the simulator supplies the frequency during the sweep.

More about the ac test source

What "AC" means. Alternating current (or voltage) keeps changing direction, usually as a smooth sine wave. Its two main properties are amplitude (how big) and frequency (how many cycles per second, in hertz).

  • Two values, two jobs: the DC value (0 here) sets the steady conditions; the AC magnitude (1 V) is the test signal used only during an AC sweep.
  • Why 1 V? With a 1 V input, the output voltage equals the gain, so no division is needed to read it.
  • Real-world equivalent: a function generator on a lab bench, with its frequency dial turned slowly while you watch the output on an oscilloscope. The AC sweep does this automatically at hundreds of frequencies.

Dual power supply

Two V parts plus power symbols (P)

Two 15 V sources stacked, with ground in the middle, give +15 V and −15 V. Power symbols named VCC and VEE carry them to the op amps.

More about the dual power supply

Why two supplies? An audio or sensor signal swings above and below 0 V. For the op amp's output to follow it both ways, it needs a supply above 0 V and one below.

  • Stacking: two sources in series add up. Grounding the point between them makes one end +15 V and the other −15 V: a 30 V span centred on ground.
  • Power symbols: drawing supply wires to every op amp clutters a schematic. Instead, a VCC symbol on the supply and on each op amp's top pin connects them by name.
  • The names: VCC and VEE come from transistor circuits, where the positive rail fed the collectors (C) and the negative rail the emitters (E).

Voltage probe

Probe tool (B)

Marks a node to graph. During an AC sweep, the probed node is graphed in decibels. It's how you'll read the amplifier's gain.

More about the voltage probe

What it measures. A voltage probe is the simulator's version of a meter or oscilloscope lead: it measures the voltage from that node to ground at every point of the analysis.

  • Voltage or current: clicking a wire probes voltage; clicking a part's body probes the current through the part.
  • Units follow the analysis: transient and DC sweep plots show volts; an AC sweep shows decibels, the natural unit for gain.
  • Probe only what you need. Every probe adds a trace. Too many make the plot hard to read.

How the op amp sets its gain

Both op amps in this lab are non-inverting amplifiers: the signal goes into the + input, and a pair of resistors feeds part of the output back to the − input. The gain depends only on those two resistors:

Gain = 1 + RF ÷ RIN = 1 + 20 kΩ ÷ 10 kΩ = 3

Two stages in a row multiply: 3 × 3 = 9. With a 1 V input, the output in the middle band is about 9 V.

Decibels

Gains are usually given in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale that turns multiplying into adding:

dB = 20 × log₁₀(Vout ÷ Vin)

Section 5.3

Concepts: filters, gain, decibels and log graphs

Hands-on explorers for the three ideas behind Lab 5A, plus how to read the log-scale graphs they produce.

Lab 5A's circuit combines three ideas: filters, amplifier gain and decibels. Before reading the real schematic, experiment with each one on its own. Nothing here can break, so try extreme settings. The section ends with a short guide to log-scale graphs, which you'll need to read Lab 5A's results.

Filters: letting some frequencies through

A low-pass filter passes slow signals and weakens fast ones. A high-pass filter does the opposite. Put one of each in a row and you get a band-pass filter, which is exactly what Lab 5A does. The key number for each is its corner frequency, where the output has dropped to 70.7% of the input (−3 dB).

Filter explorer

A filter is a voltage divider whose sharing changes with frequency, because a capacitor's reactance changes with frequency. Pick a filter, set R and C, then sweep the input frequency and watch the output.

Filter

Things to try

  • With the low-pass filter, move the frequency slowly from left to right. Watch the output wave shrink and slide later than the input: that slide is the phase shift.
  • Set the frequency so the gain reads −3 dB. Check that it matches the corner frequency and that R and the reactance are equal there.
  • Double C. The corner frequency halves. Double R instead: the same thing happens, because only the product R × C matters.
  • Switch to band-pass and compare the two corners with what you'll measure in Lab 5A.

Amplifier gain: making signals bigger

Op-amp gain and clipping

In a non-inverting amplifier, the gain is set by two resistors: gain = 1 + RF ÷ RIN. Change them, and the input size, and see what the output does. If the output would need more voltage than the supply can give, it clips.

Dashed red lines are the output limits, about 1.5 V inside each supply rail.

Things to try

  • Set RF = 20k and RIN = 10k (Lab 5A's values). The gain is 3.
  • Raise the gain to about 20 with a 1 V input and watch the output clip at the rails.
  • Set RF to 0. The gain becomes exactly 1: the op amp simply copies its input.

Decibels: gain on a log scale

Two stages with a gain of 3 each give 3 × 3 = 9. In decibels you add instead: 9.5 dB + 9.5 dB = 19 dB.

Decibel converter

Type in either box. Notice that multiplying by 10 always adds 20 dB, and halving the voltage always subtracts about 6 dB.

Log-scale graphs: reading axes that span decades

Lab 5A's AC sweep runs from 10 Hz to 100 kHz, a range of 10,000 to 1. On an ordinary linear axis, everything below 10 kHz would be crushed into the first tenth of the graph. A logarithmic scale fixes this by giving every factor of ten, called a decade, the same width.

The rule to remember: on a linear axis, equal distances mean equal differences (+100, +100, +100). On a log axis, equal distances mean equal ratios (×10, ×10, ×10). One consequence is that a log axis has no zero. Each step left divides by ten, so zero would be infinitely far away.

Linear ruler vs log ruler

Both rulers run up to 1,000. Slide the value and compare where it lands on each. The faint lines join the same numbers on the two rulers.

Four ways to draw the same data

Either axis can be linear or logarithmic, which gives four combinations. Engineers pick the one that turns the data into a shape that is easy to read, ideally a straight line. Try each combination on four graphs you'll meet in electronics.

Watch the names. "Log-linear" and "linear-log" mean different things in different books: some name the X axis first, others the Y axis. Don't trust the name. Look at the gridlines instead: the axis whose gridlines bunch up toward each power of ten is the log axis.

dB is already a log. A decibel value is 20 × log₁₀ of the gain, so plotting dB on a plain linear Y axis gives the same picture as plotting the gain ratio on a log Y axis. That's why a Bode plot is technically semi-log paper but behaves like a log–log plot, and why an RC filter's roll-off is a straight line at −20 dB per decade.

Practice: read a log frequency axis

Type the frequency at the red marker. You can use SPICE shorthand such as 3k. Remember the minor gridlines between each pair of decades mark 2, 3, 4 … 9 times the decade's start, and they get closer together as you move right.

Lab 5A: reading the schematic

Follow the signal through two filters and two amplifiers.

Open 5A · AC sweep of a band-pass amplifier from the lab list. The circuit is already drawn; read it one stage at a time, left to right, the way the signal travels. Choose a stage to highlight it, or select any part for details.

The whole circuit: the signal enters at vin, passes a high-pass filter, is amplified by U1, passes a low-pass filter, and is amplified again by U2 before reaching OUT.

Select any part for its name, value and role.

Putting the stages together

Each filter has a corner frequency where it starts cutting the signal. Together they make a band-pass response:

  • Below the high-pass corner (about 67 Hz from R1 and C1), C1 blocks the signal, so the gain falls.
  • Between the corners, both amplifiers work at full gain: 3 × 3 = 9, or about 19 dB.
  • Above the low-pass corner (about 17.9 kHz from R2 and C2), C2 shunts the signal to ground, so the gain falls again.

These are predictions from the part values. The lab asks you to measure the real values from your simulation.

Section 5.5

Lab 5A: AC sweep and output

Set up the sweep, read a log-scale graph, and find the cutoff frequencies.

An AC sweep feeds the circuit a 1 V sine wave at one frequency, records the output, steps to the next frequency, and repeats. The result is a graph of gain against frequency, called a frequency response.

Set up the Analysis panel

Try the settings here first, then enter them in Q Circuits.

Analysis (Lab 5A)

Points/decade is how many frequencies are calculated in each factor of ten. From 10 Hz to 100 kHz is four decades, so 101 points per decade gives about 400 points: plenty for a smooth curve.

  1. Type LAB 05A in the circuit name box.
  2. In the Analysis panel choose AC sweep: Start Hz 10, Stop Hz 100k, Points/decade 101.
  3. Pick Probe (B) and click the OUT wire. An AC probe is plotted in dB.
  4. Press Run simulation.

Explore the output

This graph uses the same circuit and sweep. Move the pointer over the plot, or focus it and use the arrow keys, to read values the way the Q Circuits legend does.

X gridlines

Cursor: move over the graph.

Reading the graph

  • X axis: frequency on a logarithmic scale. Every factor of ten takes the same width, which is why 10 Hz to 100 Hz looks as wide as 10 kHz to 100 kHz. The faint minor gridlines between decades mark 20, 30 … 90 of each decade; they're unevenly spaced, so read carefully.
  • Y axis: V(OUT) in dB. Since the input is 1 V (0 dB), this is also the gain.
  • Shape: a flat top with a roll-off on each side: the band-pass response predicted in the last section.
  1. Hover the flat top in Q Circuits. The legend reads the value under the cursor: that's the mid-band gain.
  2. Subtract 3 dB from it.
  3. Hover left until the trace falls to that level: the lower −3 dB frequency.
  4. Hover right until it falls to the same level: the upper −3 dB frequency.
  5. Enter all three under Your readings (suffixes work: 17.9k) and press Check my work.

Practice. Use the graph above to find the two frequencies where the trace sits 3 dB below its flat top, then check your method. Read your lab answers from your own Q Circuits run.

On this graph they're close to 66 Hz and 17.9 kHz, matching the filter corners worked out from R1·C1 and R2·C2. Your Q Circuits values should be close to these.

Lab 5B: transient analysis

Watch a Zener diode circuit over time, with current and voltage in separate panes.

Open 5B · Transient with current and voltage. It uses a transient analysis: instead of sweeping frequency, the simulator steps through time and calculates every voltage and current at each instant, so the result looks like an oscilloscope screen.

The circuit

Select the source, diode, resistor or probe.

New part: the diode

A diode is a one-way valve for current. The triangle points in the direction current can flow: from the anode into the cathode (the bar). It only starts conducting once about 0.7 V is across it in that direction.

D1 uses the D1N750 model, a Zener diode, drawn with a bent bar. It behaves like a normal diode forward, but it also conducts backward once the reverse voltage reaches its breakdown voltage, 4.7 V.

More about diodes and Zener diodes

How it works. A diode is a PN junction: P-type and N-type semiconductor joined together. Forward voltage (anode more positive) pushes charge across the junction once it exceeds about 0.6–0.7 V for silicon. Reverse voltage widens a barrier at the junction, so almost no current flows.

  • Uses: rectifiers that turn AC into DC, protection against connecting a battery backwards, and signal clipping. Lab 5B's circuit is a half-wave rectifier: it passes only the positive half of each cycle.
  • Zener diodes are built to break down at a precise reverse voltage without damage, so they're used as voltage references and simple regulators. The 1N750 breaks down at 4.7 V.
  • On a real part, a painted band marks the cathode, matching the bar on the symbol.
  • Forward drop grows slowly with current: about 0.75 V at 4 mA for this model, which is why "about 0.7 V" is a good estimate.

Diode curve explorer

The blue curve is the D1N750's current for each voltage across it. The dashed red load line shows what the source and 1k resistor allow. The circuit settles where they cross. Move the source voltage to follow one cycle of Lab 5B's sine wave.

New part: the sine source

VS's value is SIN(0 5 1k): offset 0 (centred on 0 V), amplitude 5 (swings to +5 V and −5 V), and frequency 1k (1,000 cycles per second, so one cycle lasts 1 ms).

Set up the Analysis panel

Analysis (Lab 5B)

Stop time is how long the simulated experiment lasts. Time step is the largest jump in time between calculations; the lab checks that yours is 0.01 ms or smaller. Smaller steps give smoother waveforms but take longer.

  1. Type LAB 05B in the circuit name box.
  2. In the Analysis panel choose Transient: Stop time 5m, Time step 0.01m.
  3. Pick Probe (B) and click the body of R1. That graphs the current into R1's top end.
  4. With Probe still active, click the IN wire to add V(IN). Volts and amps get their own panes.
  5. Press Run simulation. Enter the peak current in R1 and the most negative current you can see, typing milliamps with an m (for example 4.2m). Then press Check my work.

Reading the graph

  • Five pulses in 5 ms. One per 1 ms cycle.
  • Positive half-cycles: IN is positive, the diode is forward-biased, and current flows. You can predict the peak: (5 V − about 0.75 V) ÷ 1 kΩ. The pulses are narrower than half a cycle because nothing flows until the input passes about 0.7 V.
  • Negative half-cycles: the diode blocks, so the current is almost zero. Look closely near each negative peak: the trace dips below zero because −5 V slightly exceeds the 4.7 V breakdown. That's the "most negative current" the lab asks for. Turn off breakdown to see an ordinary diode, or raise the amplitude to 10 V to see the Zener conduct hard.
  • Two panes: with V(IN) above, you can see that each current pulse lines up with the top of the input wave.

Section 5.7

Lab 5C: operating point and net aliases

Name nodes, calculate steady DC voltages, and show them on the schematic.

Open 5C · Bias point and node voltages. It asks the simplest question a simulator can answer: with everything steady, what's the voltage at each node? That's an Operating point analysis (PSpice calls it Bias Point). Nothing is swept, so the plot stays empty; the answers go on the schematic instead.

Naming nodes with net aliases

A net alias is a name attached to a wire. It gives a node a meaningful name such as IN or OUT, and the netlist uses that name instead of a number. Aliases also connect: two wires with the same alias are the same node, even with no wire drawn between them. Power symbols work the same way, which is how VCC and VEE reached the op amps in Lab 5A.

  1. Type LAB 05C in the circuit name box.
  2. Pick Net alias (K) and click the wire between VS and R1. Type IN and press Enter.
  3. Place a second alias on the wire between R2 and R4, named OUT.
  4. In the Analysis panel choose Operating point.
  5. Press Run simulation, then Show DC voltages. Every node voltage appears on the sheet.
  6. Enter the voltage at OUT and at the junction of R1, R2 and R3 under Your readings, then press Check my work.

Try it

Change any value (numbers with SPICE suffixes), run, then show the voltages. The currents button is an extra for this lesson: Q Circuits prints voltages, and you can work out the currents with Ohm's law.
