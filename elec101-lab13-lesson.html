Section 13.1

Multiplexing

Sending many signals over one line, and a round-trip demo.

Optional lab. The handout marks Lab 13 as optional: it must be completed to be eligible for a grade of B.

Sending 16 signals over a long distance on 16 separate wires is expensive. A multiplexer (mux) sends them over one line instead, taking turns: first D0, then D1, and so on. This is time-division multiplexing, a kind of parallel-to-serial conversion. At the far end a demultiplexer (demux) routes each moment of the incoming signal back to its own line: serial-to-parallel.

Both ends must agree on whose turn it is. That's what the four select lines S3 to S0 do: together they count from 0 to 15 (2⁴ = 16 choices).

Section 13.2

Buses, bus entries and ports

How signals join a bus by name, and when lines connect.

A bus is a group of signals drawn as a single thick line, to keep busy schematics readable. It isn't a wire: the signals on it stay separate and join it by name.

Connecting to buses and wires
You drawConnected?
A wire that ends on another wireYes (junction dot)
A bus that ends on another busYes
A wire that ends on a busNo. Use a bus entry and a net name
A bus entry from a wire named D3 to the bus D[0:15]Yes: D3 is one of the bus's signals
Two bus entries at the same point on a busOnly if their wires have the same name
Two wires or objects with the same net nameYes, wherever they are

The Q Circuits tools

  • Bus (Y) draws the thick line. A net alias on the bus can take a bus name such as D[0:15], meaning signals D0 to D15.
  • Bus entry (Digital tab) is the short diagonal. Put its diagonal end on the bus and run a wire from its other end; then name that wire (D0, D1 …). ⇆ (Mirror horizontally) points an entry the other way.
  • Port (Digital tab) is PSpice's PORTLEFT/PORTRIGHT: a named connection drawn as an arrow. Rotate it 180° to point the other way.
  • A wire run straight onto a bus is flagged under Parts and connections.

How the pieces fit

  1. Two 8-line muxes make one 16-line mux. S0–S2 go to both chips. S3 enables U1 directly (active-low) and U2 through an inverter, so S3 = 0 picks from D0–D7 and S3 = 1 from D8–D15.
  2. The disabled chip outputs 0, so combining the two Z outputs with a gate passes only the selected bit. The handout uses a 7402 NOR, which also inverts it: MUXOutput = NOT D[selected].
  3. The demux uses that inversion. MUXInput goes to both of the 74154's active-low enables. When the data bit was 1, MUXInput is 0, the decoder is enabled, and the selected output goes low.
  4. The inverters undo the active-low outputs. The selected line reads 1, and every other line reads 0. When the data bit was 0, the decoder is disabled and all 16 lines read 0. Either way, the selected line matches the original bit.

Try it in the round-trip demo in 13.1.

Section 13.4

Lab 13A: the multiplexer

Draw two 74151As with a 16-line data bus.

Open 13A · 16-line multiplexer with a bus. This lab is marked on the drawing alone; there's no simulation to run.

Select a chip, gate or port.

  1. Type LAB 13A in the circuit name box.
  2. Place two 74151A multiplexers, U1 above U2, from the Digital tab.
  3. Pick Bus (Y) and draw a vertical bus to the left of both. Optionally name it D[0:15] with a net alias on the bus.
  4. For each data input, place a Bus entry with its diagonal end on the bus, and run a wire from it to the input. U1's I0–I7 take D0–D7; U2's I0–I7 take D8–D15. Name each wire D0 … D15: the name is what connects a wire to the bus.
  5. Run select lines S0, S1 and S2 to both chips' S0, S1 and S2 pins, and name them. Where they cross the data wires there must be no dot.
  6. S3 goes straight to U1's E̅, and through a 7404 inverter (U3A) to U2's E̅.
  7. Wire both Z outputs into a 7402 NOR (U4A), and its output to a Port named MUXOutput. The Z̅ outputs stay open.
  8. Optionally add Text labels such as Data Bus and Select Inputs, as the handout does. Then press Check my work.

Handout naming. The handout's text calls the select inputs A0–A3, but its figure labels them S0–S3. The Q Circuits exercise follows the figure: use S0–S3.

Section 13.5

Lab 13B: the demultiplexer

Draw a 74154 and sixteen inverters back onto a bus.

Open 13B · 16-line demultiplexer with a bus. It's also marked on the drawing alone.

Select the decoder, the port, or the first inverter.

  1. Type LAB 13B in the circuit name box.
  2. Place a 74154 decoder, U1. Wire its A, B, C and D inputs to wires named S0, S1, S2 and S3.
  3. Place a Port, rotate it 180° so it points right, name it MUXInput, and wire it to both G̅1 and G̅2.
  4. Place sixteen NOT (7404) inverters, one per output. The handout names them U2A–U2F, U3A–U3F and U4A–U4D. Standing them upright (press O) keeps the drawing narrow, as the handout's does.
  5. Wire each output Yk to its own inverter, and each inverter's output to a wire named Dk.
  6. Draw a vertical bus on the right. Each Dk wire reaches it through a Bus entry (⇆ points the entries the other way).
  7. Where wires cross there must be no dot. Press Check my work.

About the inverter names. The handout's text and figure name the 7404s differently. The check doesn't look at the names, only that each Yk passes through its own inverter to Dk.

Section 13.6

Lab 13C: MOD 10 counter

A synchronous decade counter, drawn and simulated.

Open 13C · Glitchless MOD 10 counter. It's Lab 11D's synchronous counter with extra gating, so it counts 0 to 9 and starts again: a decade, or BCD, counter (modulus 10).

The handout only asks you to draw 13C. The Q Circuits exercise also runs it, a natural follow-on to 11D, and asks you to read the count at three moments.

How it stops at 9

A plain 4-bit counter would go 9 → 10. Two tricks prevent that. QB's toggle condition includes QD̅, so at 9 (1001) QB stays 0. And QD's condition includes QD · QA, which is true only at 9, so QD turns off. Together, 1001 becomes 0000. Because every flip-flop changes on the same clock edge, the counter never passes through 10 even briefly: that's what “glitchless” means.

Build it in Q Circuits

  1. Type LAB 13C in the circuit name box.
  2. Place four 7473s (U1A, U1B, U2A, U2B), four 7408 ANDs (U3A–U3D) and a 7432 OR (U4A). Name the Q outputs QA, QB, QC and QD.
  3. Place a $D_HI on U1A's J and K, and tie every CLR to it. Place a DigClock, DSTM1 (OFFTIME .5m, ONTIME .5m, STARTVAL 1, OPPVAL 0), and run its wire, named CLOCK, to every CLK.
  4. U3A takes QA and QD's Q̅ (U2B's Q̅ output); it drives U1B's J and K.
  5. U3B takes U3A's output and QB; it drives U2A's J and K.
  6. U3C takes QD and QA. U3D takes U3B's output and QC. U4A takes both, and drives U2B's J and K.
  7. Transient: Stop time 12m, Time step 0.1m, Initialize flip-flops to 0. Probe CLOCK, QD, QC, QB and QA, and run.
  8. Read QD QC QB QA as a binary number for the questions, and press Check my work.
