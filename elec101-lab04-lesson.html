Section 4.1

What a schematic is for

Why schematics matter, the three quantities you need, and the basic moves on the Q Circuits sheet.

Every lab in this course starts with a schematic: a drawing that shows which parts are in a circuit and how they are connected. The simulator reads the schematic and works out what the circuit would do. If the drawing is wrong, the answer is wrong, so learning to read and draw schematics carefully is the first skill.

Why draw a schematic?

A schematic is the shared language of electronics. Before anyone builds, repairs or simulates a circuit, they work from its schematic, because it answers one question clearly: what is connected to what? It has several jobs:

  • A plan for building. A technician can wire a circuit on a breadboard or circuit board by following the schematic connection by connection.
  • A way to communicate. Engineers anywhere in the world can read the same symbols. A schematic replaces pages of description such as "the left end of the first resistor connects to the positive side of the battery".
  • The input to a simulation. Q Circuits reads your drawing and turns it into a netlist, a text list of parts and the nodes they connect to, which the simulator solves. You can see it in the Netlist panel.
  • A guide for troubleshooting. When a real circuit misbehaves, you use the schematic to decide where to measure and what reading to expect.
  • A permanent record. Months later, you or someone else can understand the design without taking the hardware apart.

Because the schematic drives everything that follows, a small drawing mistake (a missing connection, a wrong value) becomes a wrong simulation, a wrong build, or hours of troubleshooting. That's why Lab 4 is all about getting drawings right.

What a schematic doesn't tell you. Where parts sit on the drawing has nothing to do with where they sit in real life. Wire lengths, part sizes and physical layout aren't shown; only connections and values are. Neatness still matters, though: a clean layout is how people read it correctly.

Symbols, not pictures

A schematic doesn't show what parts look like or where they sit on a circuit board. Each part is a standard symbol, and the lines between them are wires: perfect conductors with no resistance. Because of that, a wire can be any length or shape; only what it connects matters.

  • A node is every point joined together by wire. All points on a node are at the same voltage.
  • A junction dot marks where three or more connections meet. Two wires that cross without a dot are not connected.
  • The ground symbol marks node 0, the 0 V reference that every other voltage is measured from.
  • Each part has a reference designator (R1, V1, Q1) and a value (1k, 12, Q2N3904).

Section 4.2

The parts in Lab 4

Symbols and values for every part you'll use, plus how the simulator reads numbers.

Lab 4 uses only a handful of parts. Learn their symbols now; you'll see them in every lab that follows.

Voltage source

Toolbar: V

A steady push of voltage, like a battery. The longer plate is the + side. Its value is typed as DC 12 for 12 volts.

More about the voltage source

How it works. A source turns some other form of energy into electrical energy. A battery uses a chemical reaction; a bench power supply converts wall power. Either way, it holds a fixed voltage between its terminals and supplies whatever current the circuit draws.

Polarity matters. Current leaves the + terminal, flows through the circuit, and returns to the − terminal. Turn the source upside down and every voltage in the circuit changes sign.

  • In the lab you'll see DC 12 (12 V steady), DC 15, and later sources that change over time, such as SIN(0 5 1k).
  • A source with no value can't be simulated; Q Circuits warns "has no value".
  • Two sources wired directly in parallel can't both set the voltage, so Q Circuits refuses to run that circuit.

Resistor

Toolbar: R

Opposes current. The value is in ohms and uses a number plus a scale letter: 1k is 1,000 Ω.

More about the resistor

How it works. A resistor is made of a material that conducts poorly, such as carbon film or metal film. As current passes through, some electrical energy turns into heat.

  • Jobs in a circuit: limit current (for example, to protect an LED or a transistor's base) and divide a voltage into smaller pieces.
  • No polarity: it works the same either way round.
  • Tolerance: real resistors are within a stated percentage of their value, commonly ±5% or ±1%. Simulated ones are exact.
  • Power rating: real resistors can only get rid of so much heat, commonly ¼ watt. Power is P = V × I.
  • Color code: real resistors show their value in colored bands. Brown–black–red means 1, 0, then two zeros: 1,000 Ω (1k).
  • Standard values: resistors come in set steps such as 1.0, 1.5, 2.2, 3.3, 4.7 and 6.8, times powers of ten.

NPN transistor

Toolbar: NPN, model Q2N3904

A three-lead semiconductor used as a switch or amplifier. Its model, chosen in the Selected part panel, tells the simulator how it behaves.

More about the npn transistor

How it works. An NPN transistor is a sandwich of three semiconductor layers: N-type, P-type, N-type. The thin middle P layer is the base. A small current into the base lets a much larger current flow from collector to emitter, typically 100 times larger or more.

  • Base–emitter drop: the base–emitter junction behaves like a diode. It needs about 0.7 V before current flows.
  • As a switch: no base current means off; enough base current means fully on. This is how a microcontroller drives a motor or relay.
  • As an amplifier: a small changing signal at the base becomes a larger copy at the collector. You'll build these in Lab 12.
  • Part names: 2N3904 is the real part, a small plastic package with three legs. Q2N3904 is the simulator's model of it.
  • NPN vs PNP: on a PNP symbol the arrow points in, and the currents flow the other way.

Ground

Tool: Ground (G)

Node 0, the 0 V reference. Every simulated circuit needs at least one.

More about the ground

What it means. Voltage is always measured between two points. Ground is the point everyone agrees to call 0 V, so "the voltage at OUT" really means "the voltage from OUT to ground".

  • Ground doesn't have to be connected to the earth. In a battery-powered device it's usually just the common return wire, often the − side of the battery.
  • Every ground symbol on a sheet is the same node, even without a wire between them. That lets you draw several grounds to keep a drawing tidy.
  • It's usually drawn at the bottom of the schematic, with voltages increasing toward the top.

Wire and junction

Tool: Wire

Wires join parts. A dot shows three or more connections meeting at one node. An open circle marks an end that isn't connected.

More about the wire and junction

Ideal wires. A schematic wire has no resistance, so every point along it is at the same voltage. That's why its length and shape don't matter.

  • Crossing is not connecting. Two wires that cross without a dot are separate. In Q Circuits a wire only connects where it ends on a pin or another wire.
  • Good practice: avoid four wires meeting at one dot. Draw two separate T-junctions instead; a dot is easy to miss when printed.
  • Right angles only: Q Circuits draws horizontal and vertical wires, the professional standard (orthogonal wiring).

Section 4.4

Naming your sheet

Why every drawing needs a title, and where it goes in Q Circuits.

A schematic needs a label that says what it is. In PSpice that's a title block: a bordered box in the lower-right corner of the drawing, the frame this lesson's header is modelled on. In Q Circuits the circuit name box at the top of the page does the same job.

Name every lab sheet

The first task in every lab is to type the lab's name into the circuit name box. The first check in every lab looks for it.

  1. Open the lab from the lab list. Its starting sheet is named Untitled circuit.
  2. Click the circuit name box and replace the text with the lab's name, for example LAB 04A.
  3. Press Enter or click the sheet.

The check accepts small variations such as Lab 04A or LAB 4A, but the lab number and letter must match the lab you have open.

Why it matters. The name travels with the circuit when you save it, share a link, or export a PNG, so anyone who sees the drawing knows which lab it belongs to.

Lab 4A: fixing wiring errors

Spot five mistakes in a broken drawing, then fix them in Q Circuits.

Open 4A · Fix the wiring errors from the lab list (under ELEC 101 · Lab 4). The sheet is the handout's lab04a circuit with its mistakes left in. Q Circuits gives you three kinds of clue: dots where connections shouldn't meet, open circles on loose ends, and the warnings under Parts and connections.

Find the errors

Try it here first. Select any spot on the drawing you think looks wrong. There are five problems to find.

Found 0 of 5

Select a spot on the schematic to inspect it.

    Fix it in Q Circuits

    1. Type LAB 04A in the circuit name box.
    2. Click the source once. The top one, V2, is selected. Press Delete. The Parts table should now list only V1.
    3. Double-click V1 and type DC 12.
    4. ⌘-drag (Ctrl-drag on Windows) R1 up out of the way. Click the bare wire underneath and press Delete. Drag R1 back onto the gap.
    5. Draw a wire from Q1's collector (the top lead) across and down to the wire feeding the base, so collector and base are joined.
    6. Pick Ground (G) and place it on the bottom wire.
    7. Make sure the Analysis panel is on Operating point, press Run simulation, and enter the voltage at Q1's emitter under Your readings.
    8. Press Check my work. Fix anything marked ✗ and check again.

    Reading the corrected circuit

    Switch to the corrected view and select each part. Trace the path the current takes: out of V1's + terminal, through R1, into the transistor, out of the emitter, through R2 to ground, and back to V1's − terminal. A complete loop like this is a closed circuit; current can only flow if the loop is unbroken.

    Analysis and output. Lab 4 uses the simplest analysis, Operating point, which calculates the steady voltage at every node. You'll learn more about it in Lab 5. Before you run it, predict the emitter voltage: the transistor drops about 0.7 V, and the rest of the 12 V is shared equally by the two 1k resistors. Then compare your prediction with the simulation.

    Glossary

    These are the key terms from this lab. Dotted-underlined words in the lesson show their definition when you point at, tap or tab to them. The full course glossary (opens in a new tab) is searchable.

    Schematic
    A drawing of a circuit using standard symbols.
    Node
    All points joined by wire; they share one voltage.
    Junction
    A dot where three or more connections meet.
    Open end
    A wire end or pin connected to nothing, shown in Q Circuits as an open circle.
    Ground (node 0)
    The 0 V reference point for all voltages.
    Short circuit
    A wire that bypasses a part, so current avoids it.
    Open circuit
    A break in the path; no current can flow through it.
    Series
    Parts connected end to end with nothing branching between them; they carry the same current.
    Parallel
    Parts connected across the same two nodes; they share the same voltage.
    Reference designator
    A part's name on the drawing, such as R1 or Q1.
    Operating point
    The steady DC voltages in a circuit. PSpice calls this analysis Bias Point.
    Circuit name box
    Where you type the lab name. It does the job of a title block.
