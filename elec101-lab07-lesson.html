Section 7.1

What a DC sweep does

Stepping a source through a range, the settings that control it, and a sweep you can watch.

So far each simulation used fixed source values. A DC sweep asks a bigger question: how does the circuit respond as a source is turned up or down? The simulator sets the source to its Start value, solves the circuit, records every probed value, moves on by one Step, and repeats until it reaches Stop. It's like slowly turning a bench power supply's knob and writing down a meter reading at every click.

Section 7.2

Lab 7A: sweeping a series circuit

Draw the circuit, set up the sweep, and read a straight-line result.

Open 7A · DC sweep. You'll draw the series circuit from Lab 6A again and sweep its source from −12 V to +12 V.

Select a part or the probe.

Draw and set up

  1. Type LAB 07A in the circuit name box.
  2. Draw the series circuit: R1 = 100 across the top, R2 = 300 upright on the right, and VS = DC 12 on the left with + up. Ground the bottom wire.
  3. Place net aliases: A where VS meets R1, and B where R1 meets R2.
  4. In the Analysis panel choose DC sweep: Source VS, Start -12, Stop 12, Step 0.1.
  5. Pick Probe (B), click the B node, then press Run simulation. The plot is V(B) against VS.
  6. Hover the two ends of the line and enter V(B) at both ends of the sweep, then press Check my work.
Analysis (Lab 7A)

Reading the result

  • It's a straight line through zero. The circuit is linear: V(B) is always the same fraction of VS. Double VS and V(B) doubles; set VS to 0 and V(B) is 0.
  • Its slope is that fraction. For a divider it's R2 ÷ (R1 + R2).
  • Negative source, negative output. When VS is below zero, the current reverses and V(B) goes negative too.
  • Axis range. The handout asks you to adjust the Y axis so the result is easy to read. In Q Circuits, open Axis ranges under the plot to set the Y limits (for example −10 to 10), or leave them blank for automatic. Make sure the whole line, from −9 V to +9 V, is visible.

Predict first. What is V(B) when VS = 12 V? (At VS = −12 V it's the same number with a minus sign.)

V(B) = VS × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2) = 12 × 300 ÷ 400 = 9 V. At VS = −12 V it's −9 V. The slope is 300 ÷ 400 = 0.75.

Section 7.3

Lab 7B: the differential probe

Measure the voltage across one part instead of from ground.

A normal voltage probe measures from a node to ground. Sometimes you want the voltage across one part, between two nodes that are both above ground. That's what a differential probe does.

v(A,B) = V(A) − V(B)

In Lab 7B you measure across R1, from A (the + side) to B (the − side).

Select the differential probe or a part.

Try the probe

Do it in Q Circuits

  1. Open 7B · Differential probe. It offers to copy your 7A circuit; accept, then rename it LAB 07B.
  2. Keep the same DC sweep: Source VS, Start −12, Stop 12, Step 0.1.
  3. Pick Diff probe (X). Click A first (the + side), then B (the − side). If a ground-referenced probe is still on B, remove it with the Probe tool.
  4. Run. The trace is v(A,B), the drop across R1. Enter its value at VS = 12 and press Check my work.

Predict first. What is v(A,B), the voltage across R1, when VS = 12 V?

V(A) is the full 12 V and V(B) is 9 V (from 7A), so v(A,B) = 12 − 9 = 3 V. Or directly: 12 × R1 ÷ (R1 + R2) = 12 × 100 ÷ 400 = 3 V. The two drops, 3 V and 9 V, add up to the source.

Section 7.4

Lab 7C: parameters and parametric sweeps

Give R1 several values in one run and compare the family of curves.

What if you want to see how the output changes for several values of R1? You could run the sweep three times, editing R1 in between. A parametric sweep does it in one run and draws every result on the same plot, a family of curves.

How it works

Think of two loops, one inside the other:

for each RVAL in 100, 200, 300:        ← parametric sweep (outer loop)
    set R1 = RVAL
    for each VS from -12 to 12 by 0.1:   ← DC sweep (inner loop)
        solve the circuit, record V(B)
    draw one line

Three things make it work together:

  1. A parameter in the value. R1's value becomes {RVAL}. The curly braces tell the simulator "look this up" instead of reading a number.
  2. A Parameter part. It defines RVAL and gives it a default value (100). It sits anywhere on the sheet and isn't wired to anything.
  3. Parametric sweep in the Analysis panel. Tick it, name the parameter (RVAL, without braces), and give the values: Linear from 100 to 300 in increments of 100, or a Value list of 100 200 300.

Select the Parameter part or R1.

Do it in Q Circuits

  1. Open 7C · Parametric sweep, start from your 7A circuit, and rename it LAB 07C.
  2. Double-click R1's value and type {RVAL}, curly braces included.
  3. Place a Parameter part anywhere on the sheet: name RVAL, value 100.
  4. In the Analysis panel keep the DC sweep and tick Parametric sweep: parameter RVAL, Linear, Start 100, End 300, Increment 100.
  5. Probe B and run. There's one line for each value of RVAL. Enter V(B) at VS = 12 for each, then press Check my work.

Predict first. Before you run it: what is V(B) at VS = 12 V when RVAL = 200?

V(B) = 12 × 300 ÷ (200 + 300) = 12 × 0.6 = 7.2 V. Use the same method for the other two values.

Section 7.5

Lab 7D: extending the circuit

Add a second divider, move the output, and see the effect of loading.

Open 7D · Extend the swept circuit. You add a second divider after the first and move the output to it. The DC and parametric sweeps carry straight over.

Choose a node to highlight it, or select a part.

Select a part.

Do it in Q Circuits

  1. Start from your 7C circuit and rename it LAB 07D.
  2. Place R3 = 150 across, to the right of the B corner. Press O and place R4 = 150 upright, below and right of R3.
  3. Wire the old B corner to R3, R3 to R4, and R4's bottom down to the ground wire.
  4. Drag the B alias to the corner between R3 and R4. The corner it left, between R1 and R2, now has no name, and that's fine.
  5. Keep the DC and parametric sweeps. Probe B (remove the old probe if it stayed behind) and run.
  6. Enter V(B) at VS = 12 for RVAL = 100, then press Check my work.

Why the output drops: loading

R3 and R4 do two things. First, they form a new path from the middle node to ground, in parallel with R2, so the middle node's voltage falls. This is called loading. Second, R3 and R4 are equal, so B is only half of the middle node's voltage.

7C compared with 7D

Sweep type

Cursor: move over the plot.

Predict first. What is V(B) at VS = 12 V when RVAL = 100?

  1. R3 + R4 = 300 Ω, in parallel with R2 (300 Ω): 150 Ω.
  2. Middle node: 12 × 150 ÷ (100 + 150) = 7.2 V.
  3. B is half of that, because R3 = R4: 3.6 V.

Section 7.6

Reading and checking results

Habits for reading sweep plots, and fixes for common problems.

A plot is only useful if you read it carefully and trust it. Use these habits on every sweep.

Reading a DC sweep plot

  • Hover to read values. The Q Circuits legend shows the value under the cursor. Read at the exact X value the question asks for.
  • Check the ends and the middle. At VS = 0, a resistor circuit should read 0 V.
  • Check the shape. Resistor-only circuits give straight lines. A curve means something non-linear, such as a diode or transistor.
  • Compare with a quick estimate. If the simulation disagrees with your hand calculation, find out which one is wrong.

Glossary

Dotted-underlined words in the lesson show their definition when you point at, tap or tab to them. The full course glossary (opens in a new tab) is searchable.

DC sweep
Steps a source's DC value and plots the results against it.
Step
The spacing between swept values (PSpice: Increment).
Linear circuit
Output proportional to input; a straight-line sweep.
Slope
How much the output changes per unit of input.
Differential probe
Measures between two nodes: v(A,B) = V(A) − V(B).
Parameter
A named value, used in a part value as {NAME}.
Parametric sweep
Repeats an analysis for each parameter value.
Family of curves
One trace per parameter value on the same plot.
Loading
Extra parts drawing current from a node and lowering its voltage.
