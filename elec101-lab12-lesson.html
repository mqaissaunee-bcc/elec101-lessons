Section 12.1

Amplifiers and frequency response

What Lab 12 measures, and the five circuits at a glance.

An amplifier makes a small signal bigger. In Lab 12 you'll build amplifiers two ways: from a discrete transistor with resistors and capacitors, and from an integrated op amp. Then you'll compare their frequency responses with an AC sweep, as in Lab 8.

What to read from an amplifier's Bode plot

  • Mid-band gain: the flat top of the curve, in dB.
  • Lower and upper −3 dB frequencies: where the gain has dropped 3 dB below mid-band at each end. Between them is the bandwidth.
  • What limits each end: at low frequencies, coupling and bypass capacitors; at high frequencies, the transistor's own tiny internal capacitances, or the op amp's limited speed.

Each Lab 12 exercise asks for two readings: the mid-band gain and the upper −3 dB frequency. SPICE suffixes work in the answer boxes: 4.5meg is 4,500,000 Hz.

Section 12.2

The common-emitter amplifier

Biasing a transistor, and what each resistor and capacitor does.

Lab 4 introduced the NPN transistor. Here it becomes a common-emitter amplifier: the input goes to the base, the output comes from the collector, and the emitter is common to both (connected to ground for AC).

Biasing: setting the operating point

A transistor only amplifies when it's partly on. The resistor network sets that DC operating point:

  1. R1 and R2 divide VCC: 18 V × 10k ÷ 72k ≈ 2.5 V at the base.
  2. The emitter sits one diode drop lower, about 1.8 V, so about 1.8 V ÷ (RE1 + RE2) ≈ 3.4 mA flows.
  3. Nearly all of it flows through RC too, dropping about 9 V, so the collector sits near the middle of the supply. The signal can then swing both ways.

The AC side

  • C1 and C2 are coupling capacitors: they pass the signal but block DC.
  • CE bypasses RE2 for AC, so only RE1 limits the gain.
  • The voltage gain is roughly (RC ‖ RL) ÷ (re + RE1), where re ≈ 25 mV ÷ IE is the transistor's own small emitter resistance.

Lab 12B: two transistor stages

Cascading stages, adding decibels, and loading.

Open 12B · Two-stage amplifier. Two common-emitter stages in cascade: the first stage's output becomes the second stage's input.

Choose a stage to highlight it, or select a part.

Select a part.

Gains multiply; decibels add

If stage one multiplies the signal by 18 and stage two by 5, the total is 18 × 5 = 90. In decibels that's 25.1 dB + 14.0 dB = 39.1 dB: adding is easier than multiplying, which is one reason engineers like dB. Stage two also loads stage one: its bias resistors and base appear in parallel with RC1, so stage one's gain is a little lower than 12A's.

  1. Open 12B and, if you like, accept the offer to start from your 12A circuit. Rename it LAB 12B, and rename the first stage's parts RC1, RE1A, RE1B, CE1.
  2. Add the second stage: R3 62k and R4 10k bias Q2 (Q2N2222); RC2 2700; RE2A 200 and RE2B 330 with CE2 47u across RE2B.
  3. Couple Q1's collector to Q2's base through C2 = 2u. Q2's collector drives OUT through C3 = 2u into RL = 1800.
  4. Same AC sweep. Probe OUT and run. Set Axis ranges X 100 to 10MEG and Y 20 to 40.
  5. Enter the mid-band gain and upper −3 dB frequency, then press Check my work.

Section 12.5

Op-amp amplifiers and the LM324

Inverting and non-inverting gain, gain-bandwidth, and the LM324 model.

An op amp (Lab 5) packs a complete multi-stage amplifier into one chip. With two resistors you set its gain precisely, with no bias network to design. Lab 14B will show you what's inside.

The two basic op-amp amplifiers
Inverting (12C)Non-inverting (12D)

Gain−RF ÷ R1 = −101 + RF ÷ R1 = 11

Input resistanceR1 (1 kΩ)Very high

Gain and bandwidth trade off

An op amp's gain × bandwidth is roughly constant: its gain-bandwidth product. For the LM324 it's about 1 MHz. The bandwidth is set by the noise gain, 1 + RF ÷ R1, which is 11 for both 12C and 12D: so both roll off near 1 MHz ÷ 11 ≈ 90 kHz. That's far lower than the transistor amplifier's megahertz bandwidth, the price of the op amp's convenience.

The LM324 model in Q Circuits

  • The handout has you open the PSpice Model Editor and type in the LM324 macromodel. In Q Circuits that exact model is built in: the LM324 part's Model field defaults to LM324 (PSpice macromodel). Leave it there; the lab marks the Behavioral option wrong.
  • The handout mirrors the op amp so pin 2 (−) faces up. In Q Circuits the − input is already on top, so no mirroring is needed.
  • The op amp needs power: wire its top supply pin to VCC and its bottom pin to VEE with power symbols, from a split ±18 V supply (VS1 and VS2, grounded where they meet).

Predict first. Lab 12D: what is the gain of the non-inverting amplifier in dB?

Gain = 1 + RF ÷ R1 = 1 + 10 = 11. In dB: 20 × log₁₀(11) = 20.8 dB.

12E: two −10 stages give +100 (the two inversions cancel), which is 40 dB. Each stage has the same ~90 kHz corner, so together they're 3 dB down at about 0.64 of that.

Glossary

Dotted-underlined words in the lesson show their definition when you point at, tap or tab to them. The full course glossary (opens in a new tab) is searchable.

Amplifier
A circuit that makes a signal larger.
Common-emitter amplifier
A transistor amplifier with input at the base and output at the collector.
Bias
The DC voltages and currents that put a transistor in its working range.
Coupling / bypass capacitor
Passes AC between stages / shorts a resistor for AC.
Mid-band gain
The flat gain between the −3 dB frequencies.
Bandwidth
The frequency range between the −3 dB points.
Cascade
Stages connected one after another.
Gain-bandwidth product
An op amp's roughly constant gain × bandwidth.
